# Facts in the film

| On screen / in the voice-over | Source |
|---|---|
| 1959: at the American National Exhibition in Moscow (25 July – 4 September 1959), Pepsi executive Donald Kendall got Soviet leader Nikita Khrushchev to drink Pepsi (via Vice President Nixon); the moment was photographed | Wikipedia, *American National Exhibition*; History.com (C. Klein); RFE/RL; Bloomberg obituary (2020) |
| In 1959 Kendall headed Pepsi's international operations; he ran the company from the 1960s (CEO of PepsiCo through the 1972 and 1989 deals). The voice-over's "Pepsi's boss" in 1959 simplifies this | Bloomberg obituary; Ripley's; Encyclopedia.com |
| 1972: the Soviets wanted Pepsi but the rouble could not be traded abroad, so the deal was a barter: cola concentrate for the exclusive US rights to sell Stolichnaya vodka ("WORTHLESS ABROAD" = not convertible) | VinePair; History.com; The Takeout; University of Oregon paper (scholarsbank) |
| 1989: to renew and expand the deal, the Soviet Union traded PepsiCo 17 old submarines, a cruiser, a frigate and a destroyer (the receipt's "TOTAL: 20 SHIPS" = 17 + 3) | New York Times (1989, as reported by History.com); History.com; Foreign Policy (P. Musgrave, 27 Nov 2021) |
| Kendall told National Security Adviser Brent Scowcroft: "We're disarming the Soviet Union faster than you are" | New York Times (as reported by History.com); Atlas Obscura |
| The popular claim that this made Pepsi "the world's sixth-largest navy" is false: the ships were old and unusable, and 20 vessels would not have come close | Foreign Policy (2021); Lead Stories fact check (May 2022); MEL Magazine |
| The ships were passed on almost at once, through a Norwegian partner, to be scrapped | Foreign Policy (2021); History.com |
| 1990: a new deal valued at about $3 billion (the Soviets were to build tankers and freighters for Pepsi to sell or lease); it fell apart when the Soviet Union dissolved in December 1991 | History.com; MEL Magazine; New York Times (1990) |

Dramatisation: the cartoon characters are stylised stand-ins with no real likeness, and none is named on screen (the businessman, "the Soviet
leader", a ministry official, an admiral, a White House adviser). The brass key, the captain's cap, the phone call from a submarine (the
remark was made in conversation), the model warship, the periscope snapping, the store and the receipt are illustrations of the facts above.
On-screen branding is generic ("COLA", "VODKA"); no real logo is shown. Scenes are compressed and simplified.
